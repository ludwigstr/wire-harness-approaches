World Models for Deformable Linear Objects: Benchmarking Physics‑Based and Learned Approaches for Wire Harness Manufacturing

Ludwig Strelokea, Kishan Thummara, Lukas Zehb, Annalena Hartmanna, Jörg Frankea
aFriedrich-Alexander-Universität Erlangen-Nürnberg, Institute for Factory Automation and Production Systems (FAPS), Egerlandstraße 7-9, Erlangen 91058, Germany bUniversity of Stuttgart, Institute for Control Engineering of Machine Tools and Manufacturing Units (ISW), Seidenstraße 34, Stuttgart 70174, Germany
Paper Code Overview

A benchmark of 10 physics-based and learned approaches for predicting wire harness dynamics in manufacturing, evaluated on 36 real manipulation clips with 4 cables and 16 tracked nodes each.

Abstract

The increasing automation of wire harness manufacturing creates a growing demand for models that accurately predict the behavior of flexible wires during manipulation. Research in Physical AI is advancing towards world models that enable autonomous agents to predict the consequences of their actions, yet remains largely disconnected from deformable linear object (DLO) modeling. DLO research has long developed dynamics models for various wire manipulation tasks without framing them as world models, while world model research has increasingly addressed deformable objects but has paid less attention to wires specifically.

This paper adopts a functional definition of world models to bridge the gap between DLO dynamics modeling and world model research, benchmarking representative approaches from both fields on a near-industry wire handling task. The benchmark evaluates how world and dynamic models represent and predict DLO motion, particularly how the physical properties like stiffness and rest shape, that have a strong influence on the accuracy of DLO prediction, are introduced. The benchmark establishes a common evaluation framework and provides adoption guidance for comparing physics-based, world-model, and dynamic-model approaches for DLO prediction.

Key observation. In every physics engine, the single most effective fitted parameter is a per-joint rest angle: the cable's plastic set. A cable on the board does not relax to a straight line, and approaches that model this rest shape gain the most.

Pipeline Overview

Perception & DLO Tracking

All approaches share one perception front-end that turns overhead camera recordings of real manipulation sequences into cable skeletons. Click a step for its details.

Segmentation: class map per frame

The segmentation pipeline exports, for every processed frame, the raw camera image (2000 × 2660 px) and a class map that labels cable pixels (yellow) and mover pixels (green). The tracker reads only these two images, so it needs no source video.

  • The longest run of consecutive source frames is kept and duplicates are dropped, so every step is exactly one source frame.
  • Frames are sampled at 4 fps (0.25 s per step).

Board and module detection

  • find_board locates the board bezel and gives the pixel-to-millimetre transform, so all tracks are in metres on the board plane.
  • track_harness finds the five modules per frame from the class map, the hub and the four plugs on their movers, with stable identities and the hub first.
  • The hub and plug positions are the boundary conditions (actions) handed to every approach.

Cable routes (final_v1 tracking, 1 Nov 2026)

  1. Per-frame detector (tracking_v2.py): generates diverse route candidates, uses one global length per cable, and runs Viterbi over the whole clip.
  2. TrackDLO-style tracker (track_hybrid.py): CPD/GMM with a motion-coherence prior, all four cables in one model, followed frame to frame. A chain is reset to the detector's route when it leaves the evidence or when its ends jump by more than 60 mm (recording cuts).

Thin-wire re-trace of cable 3 (magenta)

Cable 3 is a loose bundle of 2–3 thin wires that the class map misses over long stretches. track_pink.py traces it directly in the camera image:

  • thin dark lines are found with a black-hat filter;
  • the route is the cheapest path from the hub to its mover, with extra cost on the three thick cables and a pull towards the previous frame.

16 equidistant nodes per cable

Each cable is traced from the hub to its mover and resampled to 16 nodes. One clip is stored as an npz with c0..c3 of shape (T, 16, 2) in metres, node 0 at the hub, plus fps, source_frames, a per-frame reset flag and the magenta cable length.

Benchmark Setup

Each approach receives the same inputs: the initial cable configuration (node positions at frame 0) and the measured connector trajectories. The task is to predict the cable shape at every subsequent frame. Both end nodes (hub connector and plug connector) follow their measured tracks: they are the driving boundary condition, not a prediction target.

The benchmark spans 36 manipulation clips recorded on a real wire harness assembly board (27 on 21 Oct, 3 on 26 Oct and 6 on 28 Oct 2026), whole clips with no trims, 118–181 frames each and 5,853 frames in total. Each clip contains 4 cables with 16 tracked nodes.
